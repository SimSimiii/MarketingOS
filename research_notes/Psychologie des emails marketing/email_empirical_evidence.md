# Empirical evidence on what makes marketing and sales emails get opened, read, clicked, replied to and acted on

> Notes compiled 29 October 2026. **Grades:** STRONG = meta-analysis, large randomized field experiment, or replication; MODERATE = single peer-reviewed study, or large dataset with disclosed method; WEAK-FOLKLORE = vendor claim without method, or a figure only reachable through secondary sites. A "−" (e.g. MODERATE−) means borderline: method only partly disclosed, or correlational with obvious confounds. **Segments:** [B2B-COLD] cold sales/outreach; [B2B-NURTURE] B2B newsletters/nurture; [B2C] promotional/lifecycle; [SURVEY] email invitations asking strangers to act (the closest randomized analogue to a cold ask). **Open-rate flags:** [PRE-MPP] = gathered before Apple Mail Privacy Protection (effective 20 Sep 2021); [MPP-INFLATED] = gathered after it without excluding machine opens. **MarketingOS application tags:** (a) deterministic code check, (b) strategist/writer prompt instruction, (c) cold-reader/critic criterion, (d) only measurable with real send data. **Method note:** the web-search budget ran out near the end, so a few figures come only from search-result summaries of secondary sites. They are marked "(secondary)", and the primary page should be checked before quoting them.

## 1. Personalization: what peer-reviewed field experiments show

### Takeaway
The only STRONG causal evidence is that a recipient's name in the **subject line** increases opens, leads and retention (Sahni et al. 2018, randomized). Observational and lab evidence shows that **visibly using personal data without saying why** (a name greeting to a stranger, a "we know you…" message) can backfire. **Relevance-based personalization** (products or topics fitted to behaviour, role or company) has the largest and most consistent associations with response. For MarketingOS, which has no recipient-level data, the transferable rule is "fit and reason-why" rather than tokens.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG | B2C/education lead-gen, marketplace, university | PRE-MPP for opens] Sahni, Wheeler & Chintagunta (2018), *Marketing Science* 37(2):236–258.** Randomized field experiments with three partners: an online test-preparation company, MercadoLibre (a South American marketplace) and Stanford University. In the main experiment, adding the recipient's name to the subject line raised the probability of opening by ~20% (from about 9% to about 11% of emails), raised sales leads by ~31% (the Booth summary says ~30%) and cut unsubscribes by ~17% (1.2% → 1.0%). In one campaign alone that was 35 extra leads and 85 fewer unsubscribes. A discount offer increased leads **only when the email was also personalized**. The effects held from acquiring new customers through to retaining past buyers. The proposed mechanism is attention: the name is "noninformative" but increases processing of the rest of the message. Sample sizes are not given in the accessible summaries. — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/personalization-email-marketing-role-non-informative-advertising); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/open-name-why-personalized-email-subject-lines-work); [INFORMS supplement page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/suppl/10.1287/mksc.2017.1066); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ecl/stabus/3409.html)
  - App: (b) allow a first-name merge field in the subject when the sender's list has one. (a) Validate the merge-field syntax and require a fallback: MarketingOS does not send, so it outputs a placeholder, and a literal `{{...}}` must sit inside `{% raw %}` in the prompts. (d) The lift size, and whether it wears off with repeated use, can only be measured in sends.
- **[STRONG | B2C] Sahni, Zou & Chintagunta (2017), *Management Science* 63(8):2688–2705**, "Do Targeted Discount Offers Serve as Advertising? Evidence from 70 Field Experiments." Seventy randomized experiments at a large online ticket-resale platform. Emailed targeted discount offers raised spending by $3.03 (+37.2%) during the promotion window, yet **~90% of the gain did not come through redeeming the offer**. Spending was still +$1.55 in the week after the offer expired, and spilled over into genres the offer did not cover. The authors read this as emailed offers acting as advertising and reminders. — [INFORMS abstract](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2450); [SUFE abstract](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/392661); [Stanford GSB Insights](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/navdeep-sahni-email-promotions-prospective-customers-really-do-work)
  - App: (b) the strategist can treat each email as a reminder of the brand and category, not only as a vehicle for its offer. (d) magnitude.
- **[MODERATE | B2C e-commerce | observational] Wattal, Telang, Mukhopadhyay & Boatwright (2012), *Information Systems Research* 23(3):679–697**, "What's in a 'Name'?" Covers 10M+ marketing emails sent to ~600,000 customers of one online merchant (study period not stated). **Product-based personalization** (recommendations from past purchases, with no explicit mention of personal data) drew positive responses. **Explicit use of personally identifiable information** (a greeting by name) drew negative ones. The greeting penalty was weaker for customers familiar with the firm, and customers with past purchases were unaffected. The press release says "95 percent of customers responded negatively when an email ad greeted them by name" and that product recommendations "triggered positive responses in 98%". It does not explain what these percentages measure (probably individual-level estimates), so verify them in the paper before quoting. The authors advise against personalized greetings for new customers. — [Newswise press release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/dear-insert-company-name-personalized-emails-don-t-impress-customers); [abstract (SUFE)](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/407147); [Temple MIS](https://community.mis.temple.edu/blog/2012/03/09/professor-sunil-wattal-measures-the-business-value-of-personalization/)
  - App: (b) do not open cold or new-audience emails with "Dear {FirstName}". (c) Cold-reader criterion: "Does this email show it knows something about me without saying why that matters to me?"
- **[MODERATE | consumer email solicitations | lab experiments] White, Zahay, Thorbjørnsen & Shavitt (2008), *Marketing Letters* 19(1):39–50**, "Getting too personal." Highly personalized messages trigger **personalization reactance** when the firm does not explicitly justify why the offer fits the person. Consumers who see higher utility in the offer need less justification. The authors warn against concluding that marketers should abandon personalization: "there needs to be a perceived value". Sample sizes are not in the sources reached. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-007-9027-9); [University of Illinois news](https://news.illinois.edu/personal-information-in-e-mail-marketing-can-backfire-study-indicates/)
  - App: (b) whenever copy uses something known about the reader (role, past action, company event), state the reason it is relevant. (c) Criterion: "Is the fit justified?"
- **[MODERATE | personalized online ads on social media, not email] Aguirre, Mahr, Grewal, de Ruyter & Wetzels (2015), *Journal of Retailing* 91(1):34–49**, "Unraveling the Personalization Paradox." Three experiments showing that a firm's strategy for collecting information from social media determines how people react to personalized ads, through perceived vulnerability and click-through. Trust-building strategies are also tested. As I recall it, the abstract says covert collection raises vulnerability and lowers click-through, and that trust-building offsets this; I could not re-read the abstract in this session to confirm. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jouret/v91y2015i1p34-49.html)
- **[MODERATE | permission-based email of an information website | model-based] Ansari & Mela (2003), "E-Customization," *JMR* 40(2):131–145.** A statistical and optimization approach to tailoring an email's design and content to drive site traffic. The model estimates that **content targeting could raise expected click-throughs by 62%**. This is a model-based counterfactual, not a live A/B result. — [Duke PDF](https://faculty.fuqua.duke.edu/~mela/bio/papers/Ansari_Mela_2003.pdf); [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/788148)
- **[STRONG (meta-analysis) | SURVEY] Edwards et al., Cochrane review "Methods to increase response to postal and electronic questionnaires" (2009; updated 2023, CDSR Issue 11, MR000008.pub5).** For electronic questionnaires, 32 eligible studies covering 27 strategies. **Personalised invitations or e-questionnaires: OR 1.24 (95% CI 1.17–1.32; I²=41%).** — [Cochrane summary (PT)](https://www.cochrane.org/pt/MR000008/METHOD_metodos-para-aumentar-resposta-questionarios-postais-e-eletronicos); [Cochrane Library 2023](https://cochranelibrary.com/es/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.MR000008.pub5/full/zh_HANT)
- **[MODERATE− | B2B-COLD | vendor, correlational] Gong Labs (2023).** More than 30,000 prospecting emails from 250+ companies, labelled by 2,300+ classifiers. Personalization about the individual (a promotion, hobby or life event) "more than doubles" reply rate for non-managerial personas but gives only +50% for directors and above. Personalization about the company (news, product releases) **triples** reply rate for directors and above, with little correlation for lower-level buyers. Personalization about the industry (social proof, case studies) goes with +88% replies. Activity-based personalization (prior conversations, downloads, events) is headlined as "3X replies and meetings" with no figure in the body. — [Gong Labs](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/4-data-backed-ways-to-increase-your-email-reply-rate-and-book-that-meeting/)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD | MPP-INFLATED for opens] Hunter, *State of Cold Email 2026* (31M cold emails sent in 2025).**
  - Body with two custom attributes: 5.6% reply, vs 3.6% with no personalization (+56%).
  - Manually edited emails: 5.2%, vs 4.4% for fully automated ones (+18%).
  - Subject-line personalization: 35.4% open rate with one custom attribute, 40.2% with two (+14%).
  - Survey of decision makers (n not disclosed): 67% say personalization using public information makes them more likely to reply; 65% blame overly sales-focused emails and 61% blame irrelevance when they don't.

  — [Hunter](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email)
- **[MODERATE− | link-building outreach] Backlinko × Pitchbox (2019, 12M outreach emails).** Personalized subject lines: +30.5% responses. Personalized body: +32.7%. Correlational, and the article's figures are internally inconsistent in places. — [Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study)

### Inferences
- **Hierarchy of evidence.** (1) Relevance, meaning the content fits this reader and says why, is supported by observational data (Wattal), vendor data (Gong, Hunter) and lab data (White). (2) A name token in the subject line is the only token with randomized support, and only for B2C, education lead-gen and university senders, with opens measured before MPP. (3) Displaying personal data without justification is a documented risk.
- **The Sahni and Wattal results do not contradict each other.** Sahni put the name in the subject line in randomized tests; Wattal measured name greetings in the body, observationally, among a mix of familiar and unfamiliar customers. One consistent reading: the name helps capture attention in the inbox, but shown to a stranger inside the body it signals surveillance, unless familiarity or an explicit reason defuses that.
- **MarketingOS.** Personalization has to be built from audience and segment knowledge (role, company type, triggering situation), which the strategist already plans per brief. Name tokens are an optional merge field with a fallback. The cold reader is well placed to judge "why me?" and "is this creepy?", which are comprehension-type judgments (c). Gong's finding that the useful *kind* of personalization varies by seniority is a strategist instruction (b), not a gate.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and per-partner effect sizes for Sahni et al. (2018). Whether name-in-subject effects decay with repeated use or as the practice became universal. No post-MPP replication found.
- Wattal et al.'s model specification and what the 95%/98% figures actually measure.
- The paper and authorship behind Goic et al. (see section 5). Aguirre et al.'s exact effect sizes.
- No randomized evidence specific to [B2B-NURTURE] personalization was found.
- Widely repeated vendor figures were not verified here and should be treated as WEAK-FOLKLORE: Experian's "personalized promotional emails get 29% higher unique opens / 41% higher unique clicks", and "personalized subject lines are 26% more likely to be opened" (often attributed to Campaign Monitor).

## 2. Subject lines — and how reliable open-rate findings are after Apple Mail Privacy Protection

### Takeaway
Apart from the name effect above, subject-line "rules" rest on large but **correlational and mutually inconsistent** vendor datasets: optimal length is reported as 3–4 words, 5–6 words, 36–50 characters, or "no relationship at all". Emoji evidence is mixed. No rigorous email-specific evidence was found for urgency words, numbers, questions or curiosity gaps. Since **20 September 2021**, any open-based result that does not exclude Apple machine opens is unreliable. Apple accounted for 76.56% of tracked opens in September 2026, and Litmus says MPP affects ~55–60% of all opens.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE | mixed senders | PRE-MPP] Mailchimp (2012), ~12 billion sends.** No meaningful correlation between subject-line length and open or click rate overall ("as your subject line gets longer, nothing happens"). Shorter may be better for mobile readers. Among accounts where length explained much of the variance (r² > 0.5), 40% did better with longer subject lines and 60% with shorter ones. — [Mailchimp blog post, archived (Sep 2012)](https://jacobfilipp.com/maildata/this-just-in-subject-line-length-means-absolutelynothing/index.html)
- **[MODERATE− | 1:1 email expecting a reply, not cold | reply-based] Boomerang (2016), 40M+ emails** where users asked to be reminded if no reply came. Subject lines of **3–4 words** (excluding Re:/Fwd:) got the most responses, declining slowly as words were added. **Emails with no subject line got a 14% response rate**, against a sample-wide level around 50%. — [Boomerang](https://blog.boomerangapp.com/2016/02/7-tips-for-getting-more-responses-to-your-emails-with-data/)
- **[MODERATE− | link-building outreach | reply-based] Backlinko × Pitchbox (2019, 12M).** Subject lines of 36–50 characters performed best. Long subject lines beat short ones by 24.6% (summary) or 32.7% (body text); the article contradicts itself. Personalized subject lines: +30.5%. — [Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD | 2025 data; opens MPP-INFLATED] Hunter (2026 report, 31M emails).** Optimal subject length is 5–6 words (no rates given). The **"Quick question" pattern underperforms**: 2.5% reply and 28.7% open, vs 2.9% and 32.9% for other subject lines. Subject personalization: 35.4% vs 40.2% opens. — [Hunter](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email)
- **[STRONG (meta-analysis) | SURVEY] Cochrane (Edwards et al. 2023).** Having the word **"survey" in the subject line lowered response (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.67–0.97; I²=0%).** — [Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/pt/MR000008/METHOD_metodos-para-aumentar-resposta-questionarios-postais-e-eletronicos)
- **[MODERATE− | B2B media newsletter | PRE-MPP] Search Engine Journal emoji test (June–July 2020).** 3,893,391 emails across 17 campaigns to a ~110,000-subscriber list, mostly A/B tested with the emoji at the front, the emoji at the end, or no emoji. Subject lines **without** emoji "won" on open rate in 52.94% of comparisons vs 47.06%, and many wins were marginal (e.g. 11.24% vs 11.26%). Emoji versions had higher click-through in most campaigns, reported as "11 of 15" and as 64.71%, which don't match. Assignment method and significance tests are not reported. — [Search Engine Journal](https://searchenginejournal.com/emojis-in-subject-lines)
- **[STRONG for mechanism; MODERATE for numbers] Apple MPP.** Announced at WWDC21 on 7 June 2021; effective **20 September 2021** for Mail on iOS 15 and iPadOS 15, with macOS Monterey later in 2021. Apple's proxy preloads message content, including tracking pixels, at unpredictable times. This registers as an open, hides the reader's IP and location, and conceals the real open. What it breaks: open-based A/B winners, segmentation by last open, open-triggered automations, send-time optimization, and dynamic content such as countdown timers and local weather. Apple clients already made up >46% of opens in 2020 and 49.8% by the end of August 2021. Litmus predicted an inflated Apple Mail open rate of around 75% at peak adoption. As a pre-MPP baseline, Constant Contact's average open rate was 17.6% in both August and September 2021. — [Litmus blog](https://www.litmus.com/blog/apple-mail-privacy-protection-for-marketers)
- **[MODERATE] Litmus Email Client Market Share, September 2026 (>1.5B opens).** Apple 76.56%, which includes MPP opens Litmus calls "not considered reliable"; Gmail 16.10%; Outlook desktop 3.48%; Yahoo 2.35%. Litmus states that MPP "now impact[s] roughly 55–60% of all email opens", citing unnamed studies. Litmus reports separate "Apple Privacy-Impacted Opens" and excludes them from charts by default. — [Litmus market share](https://www.litmus.com/email-client-market-share); [Litmus help](https://help.litmus.com/article/405-apple-mail-opens-reported-in-email-analytics)
- **[WEAK for magnitude; mechanism documented] B2B click inflation by security scanners.** Gateways such as Mimecast and Proofpoint open embedded links to check them, sometimes again at time-of-click in a sandbox. Corporate inboxes therefore show more bot clicks than consumer webmail, and hidden links can collect large numbers of "unique clicks". The circulating percentages (e.g. 60–70% of cold-outbound clicks) come from vendor blogs with no stated method. — [Suped](https://www.suped.com/knowledge/email-deliverability/technical/understanding-and-reducing-high-proofpoint-click-events-for-email-marketers); [Arternal support](https://support.arternal.com/en/articles/15216436-smart-mail-clickthroughs-triggered-by-email-security-scanners)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD] The cold-email industry is abandoning opens altogether.** Belkins disabled open tracking for every 2025 campaign. Hunter finds reply rates of **7.4% with open tracking disabled vs 4.4% with it enabled** (+68%). This is correlational, though a deliverability mechanism is plausible. — [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates); [Hunter](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email)
- **[Not retrieved] Balakrishnan & Parekh (2014)**, "Learning to predict subject-line opens for large-scale email marketing", IEEE BigData 2014, pp. 579–584. The paper exists, but its content was not retrieved. — [CPS-VO listing](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/19735)

### Inferences
- **Length is not worth optimizing.** The datasets disagree (3–4 words for replies; 5–6 words; 36–50 characters; no correlation), and the one very large analysis found no relationship. Defensible deterministic checks (a) are narrower: block an **empty subject** (Boomerang: 14% response vs ~50%), and flag subjects long enough to be truncated on mobile. The truncation flag rests on display behaviour, not on a measured effect.
- **Clichés and genre labels underperform.** "Quick question" is in Hunter's data, and "survey" is in the Cochrane meta-analysis. By analogy, a subject that announces a generic category ("newsletter", "special offer") rather than the specific content may cost response, but this has not been tested for marketing email. This suits a critic criterion (c): "Does the subject say what *this* email is about, or only what genre it belongs to?"
- **No model can predict open rates, and nothing in the evidence suggests a cold reader can either.** The defensible proxy is comprehension: does the inbox row (sender + subject + preheader) let the reader say what this is and why it is for them? That matches the existing `clarity_gate` philosophy.
- **Open-based claims by date.** Any open-rate claim from after September 2021 should be treated as contaminated unless the source says it excluded MPP. Claims from before 2021 were pixel-based and biased by image blocking, but are at least not machine opens. The report should say "open" only with a date attached.
- **Tests of subject wording (urgency, curiosity, numbers, emoji) are (d) territory.** They need real sends, measured by clicks or downstream actions, not opens.

### Gaps
- No email-specific peer-reviewed field experiments were found on subject-line **urgency words, numbers, questions, curiosity gaps or sentiment**. Miller & Charles (2016) could not be found, and Balakrishnan & Parekh's content was not retrieved.
- A Nielsen Norman Group study reportedly found that emoji in subject lines draw attention but don't raise intent to open; the primary page could not be located before the search budget ran out.
- No experiment isolating the **preheader**, and no experiment on **sender name** (e.g. a person's name vs the brand), was retrieved; only surveys (section 7).
- The JRIM paper secondary sites cite for "up to 3 emojis increase opens" could not be verified.
- Widely repeated folklore whose original sources were not found: "47% open based on subject line alone", "69% report spam based on subject line alone", Return Path's "12.5% higher opens under 49 characters", and Phrasee's "length explains 0.1% of open variance".

## 3. Length, reading level, questions, sentiment and reply rates in sales/cold outreach

### Takeaway
All the evidence here is **vendor data, correlational and differently defined**. No peer-reviewed field experiment on B2B cold-email copy was found. Where the datasets overlap they broadly agree: short-to-medium emails (≈50–150 words), simple language, one to three questions, a lightly warm tone, and follow-ups. But in the largest cold-specific dataset (Hunter, 34M emails), **length barely matters**. Targeting and infrastructure factors (list size, custom domain, open tracking) show larger differences than copy factors. Headline "reply rates" range from 0.45% to 8.5% purely because the denominators differ.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE− | not cold: 1:1 emails whose senders expected a reply] Boomerang (2016, "over 40 million emails").** A response means an actual reply. Reading level is Flesch–Kincaid; sentiment is scored −1 to 1 by an unnamed tool. No per-bucket sample sizes or significance tests.
  - *Length:* 50–125 words is the sweet spot, all above 50% response. About 44% at 500 words, flat until ~2,000, below 35% above 2,500. A 25-word email ≈ 44%. Subject but empty body: 11%.
  - *Reading level:* 3rd grade gets 53% response vs 39% at college level (+36% relative), and +17% vs high-school level.
  - *Questions:* emails with 1–3 questions were 50% more likely to get a response than emails with none; 3 questions were 20% more likely than 8 or more.
  - *Sentiment:* slightly to moderately positive or negative emails got 10–15% more responses than neutral ones. Around +0.35 was strong; above +0.9 performed like neutral; strongly negative (below −0.9) "significantly reduced" response.
  - *Subjectivity:* more opinionated emails got more responses (no numbers).

  — [Boomerang](https://blog.boomerangapp.com/2016/02/7-tips-for-getting-more-responses-to-your-emails-with-data/)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD] Hunter word-count analysis**: 34M cold emails sent 2022–2024 through Hunter Sequences. Campaigns under 50 emails, or with reply rates above 30% or below 0.5%, were excluded; replies were not classified by sentiment. Reply rate by word count: 0–19: 3.7%; **20–39: 4.5%**; 40–59: 3.4%; 60–79: 4.0%; 80–99: 4.1%; 100–119: 3.7%; 120–139: 3.6%; 140–159: 3.5%; 160–179: ~4% (misprinted in the source); 180–199: 3.7%; 200+: 3.7%. Under 100 words averages 3.9% vs 3.7% for longer emails, which the author says "isn't spectacular". — [Hunter blog](https://hunter.io/blog/cold-email-word-count)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD | 2025 data] Hunter, *State of Cold Email 2026* (31M emails).** Reply rate is implicitly replies ÷ recipients.
  - *Overall:* average sequence reply rate 4.5% (sales 3%, marketing 6.2%, digital PR 13%, HR 7.5%); unsubscribe 1.6%; bounce 3.6%.
  - *Sequence size:* 21–50 recipients get 6.2%, vs 2.4% for 500+.
  - *Sending volume:* 20–49 emails per day per account get 5.7%.
  - *Sender domain:* custom domain 5.2% vs freemail 2.5%; Google Workspace 5.9% vs Microsoft 365 ~4%.
  - *Contacts per company:* 1–2 contacts 5.1% vs 3+ contacts 3.5%.
  - *Length:* 61–80 and 181–200 words were relatively high (exact values shown only in a chart).
  - *Timing:* day of the week had no measurable effect.

  — [Hunter](https://hunter.io/the-state-of-cold-email)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-COLD | agency client campaigns, 2025] Belkins.** 7,530,489 cold emails and 34,393 replies. Reply rate = unique replies ÷ emails sent, excluding auto-replies and bounces, with open tracking off. Belkins notes this is **not comparable with its earlier studies**, which divided by unique openers.
  - *Overall:* **0.45%**, peaking at 0.54% in February and falling to 0.35% in December.
  - *Seniority:* founders/owners 0.57%, C-level 0.42%, VPs 0.32%.
  - *Company size:* 0–10 employees 0.72%, 11–50 employees 0.49%, 10,000+ employees 0.22%.
  - *Industry:* Food & Beverage 3.47%; Construction, Financial Services, Healthcare and Legal 0.56–0.60%.
  - *Outcome:* over 1,200 appointments booked in total (≈0.016% of emails).

  — [Belkins](https://belkins.io/blog/cold-email-response-rates)
- **[MODERATE− | link-building outreach] Backlinko × Pitchbox (2019, 12M).** 8.5% of outreach emails got a response. Sending to several contacts at the same site got +93% response vs a single contact, with diminishing returns at 5+. A 3-part sequence sent to several contacts got +160% vs a single email to a single contact. — [Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study)
- **[MODERATE− | B2B-COLD | vendor; scoring rubric undisclosed] Lavender (February 2026).** 231,818 recent cold emails from ~50,000 inboxes using Lavender's coach. Auto-replies excluded; positive and negative replies both count. In the page's indexed snippet, emails graded A (score 90+) lift reply rate from a 3.4% baseline to 4.3% (+27%); this figure was not visible in the fetched page text. Linked posts: Finance shows the largest lift (+79%), Operations reaches 5.4% for A-level emails (+58%), Engineering/product 5.2%. — [Lavender](https://www.lavender.ai/blog/the-cold-email-benchmark-report)
- **[MODERATE− | B2B-COLD | vendor; outcome = meetings booked; "millions" of emails, n not given] Gong (2021 post, updated 2026).** Emails of 30–150 words are "significantly more effective in booking a meeting". ROI language lowers success by 15%. Some phrases raise replies but not meetings: **"Thoughts?" may raise reply rates but lowers meetings booked by 20%**, and **"I never heard back" raises replies but lowers meetings booked by 14%**. "Hope all is well" goes with +24% meetings booked. Mentioning a call or voicemail does not raise meetings booked. — [Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-email-stats)
- **[MODERATE | peer-reviewed, descriptive, consumer webmail] Kooti, Aiello, Grbovic, Lerman & Mantrach (2015, WWW), "Evolution of Conversations in the Age of Email Overload."** 16 billion emails exchanged by 2M+ users. When people receive more messages in a day they **reply to a smaller fraction, with shorter replies**, while responsiveness "remains intact" or even speeds up. Portable devices affect reply time and length (no figures in the abstract). — [arXiv 1504.00704](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.00704)
- **[MODERATE− | not marketing: help requests on mailing lists] Boomerang sign-off analysis (2017)**: 350,000+ threads from the archives of 20+ online communities, closings extracted by regex. Response rates: "thanks in advance" 65.7%, "thanks" 63.0%, "thank you" 57.9%, "cheers" 54.4%, "kind regards" 53.9%, "regards" 53.5%, "best regards" 52.9%, "best" 51.2%; baseline 47.5%. — [Boomerang](https://blog.boomerangapp.com/2017/01/how-to-end-an-email/)

### Inferences
- **Reply-rate figures cannot be compared across sources.** Belkins' 0.45% (per email sent, across all steps, agency lists, 2025), Hunter's 4.5% (per recipient per sequence, 2025), Lavender's ~3.4% baseline (2026), Backlinko's 8.5% (2019, link-building) and Boomerang's ~50% (not cold at all) measure different things. The report should never set them side by side without their denominators.
- **Boomerang's oft-quoted 50–125 words, 3rd-grade level and 1–3 questions come from emails people expected answers to**, a much warmer context than cold prospecting. They are the best available evidence on *reading level, questions and sentiment*, but transferring them to cold outreach is an assumption.
- **Length in cold email.** Gong (30–150 words, meetings) and Boomerang (50–125) agree on "short, not ultra-short". Hunter's flat curve (3.4–4.5% across buckets) says length is a weak lever once a message is in that range.
- **Reply is not the goal.** Gong's "Thoughts?" and "I never heard back" results show that phrases which prompt a reply can lower meetings. The outcome a campaign optimizes for (reply, positive reply, meeting) changes which copy "wins". For MarketingOS, a cold reader should judge whether the email moves a qualified reader toward the *next real step*, not whether it extracts any reply at all.
- **Confounds.** Better senders tend to write shorter, more personalized emails to smaller, better-targeted lists from warmed custom domains. Hunter's infrastructure gaps (custom domain ×2, open tracking off +68%, small sequences +158%) are larger than any copy gap reported, which suggests copy effects in these datasets partly carry targeting and deliverability effects.
- **Applications.**
  - (a) Soft bands, not blocks: cold first touch roughly 50–150 words; question count 1–3 for reply-seeking emails (flag 0 or >3); a readability score as a signal.
  - (a) The readability score must be language-specific. Flesch–Kincaid is an English formula, so French copy needs a French-calibrated formula; which one was not researched here.
  - (b) Writer: plain words, a lightly warm and non-hyped tone, avoid unlicensed ROI claims. MarketingOS's gates already require licensed numbers.
  - (c) Cold reader: "Could you answer this in one line?" and "Is the next step clear and worth taking?"
  - (d) Every reply-rate magnitude.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed randomized experiment on B2B cold-email copy** (length, reading level, questions, sentiment) was found.
- **Instantly's "Cold Email Benchmark Report 2026"** (2025 data) is widely quoted in secondary summaries: average reply 3.43%, top quartile 5.5%+, top decile 10.7%+, ~58% of replies from step 1 and 42% from follow-ups. Its primary methodology page could not be reached. Treat these as WEAK-FOLKLORE until it is.
- Woodpecker's follow-up statistics were not retrieved.
- Lavender's "3rd–5th-grade reading level gets 67% more replies" appears only on secondary sites; WEAK-FOLKLORE.
- A secondary-cited Sales.co dataset (2M+ emails, 161 campaigns, 2.09% reply, 79% of replies from the first email) could not be traced.
- No French-language or European cold-email copy data was found. Belkins' 2025 country splits (US 0.51%, UK 0.48%, Poland 1.43%) don't include France.

## 4. Calls to action, structure and format: interest vs meeting CTA, single vs multiple CTA, button vs link, plain text vs HTML, images, video, P.S., signature

### Takeaway
For B2B, the best evidence on format is HubSpot's ≥13 A/B tests (2014 data): **simpler, plainer emails won every test**, even though two-thirds of surveyed professionals *said* they preferred HTML. For B2C, image-heavy designs are normal in product categories (Constant Contact, correlational). For cold-email CTAs, Gong says an **interest-based question** outperforms asking for a specific meeting time, but the numbers are only reachable through secondary sources. The single-CTA, button-vs-link, video-thumbnail and P.S. "rules" have **no rigorous email evidence** found here. The randomized survey-invitation literature consistently supports **lowering the cost of the first step**.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE− | B2B-COLD] Gong.** Classifies cold-email CTAs as specific time, open-ended or interest, and says the **interest CTA is the "highest performing"** for cold email. The rates appear only in an image. — [Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-email-stats)
- **[WEAK-FOLKLORE | B2B-COLD | secondary] Gong's CTA study, as summarized by a third party:** 304,174 emails analyzed; interest CTAs **12% total reply vs 7% for time-request CTAs**; 15% meeting-booking rate for cold-stage interest CTAs. The same write-up cites Jason Bay's data in which **offer-based CTAs out-replied interest-based ones**. Neither figure was verified against a primary page, and Gong's 85M-email guide is gated. — [Growleads (secondary)](https://growleads.io/blog/interest-based-ctas-vs-meeting-requests-study/); [Gong gated guide](https://www.gong.io/resources/guides/how-to-master-cold-email-get-the-data-backed-guide-based-on-85-million-emails)
- **[MODERATE | B2B-NURTURE (HubSpot's own audience) | 2014 data; open findings PRE-MPP]**
  - *Method:* a 2014 survey of 1,000+ professionals; at least 13 A/B tests across database segments in several regions; click-rate data from over half a billion emails sent by HubSpot customers.
  - *Stated preference:* nearly 2/3 of survey respondents said they preferred HTML/image-based emails.
  - *Behaviour:* in the A/B tests the **simpler design won every time, with statistical significance**. GIF vs plain text: the GIF version had a 2.3% lower clickthrough rate, and plain text got **42% more clicks** overall. HTML template with images vs plain text: −21% clickthrough rate and **51% fewer clicks**. Simple vs heavy HTML: +5.3% clickthrough rate and 30% more clicks. Even one image reduced click rate. HTML lowered open rates (exact figures only in charts).
  - *HubSpot's own caveats:* the "plain-text" versions still carried links and a tracking pixel; e-commerce may differ; percent vs percentage-point is ambiguous; the data are from 2014.

  — [HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/plain-text-vs-html-emails-data)
- **[WEAK-FOLKLORE] Contrary practitioner tests.** A Yespo split test found plain text still ahead but by much less than HubSpot reported. Secondary sources say Marketo saw no difference in opens. — [Yespo](https://yespo.io/blog/plain-text-email-vs-html-new-trend-or-well-established-practice-what-more-effective)
- **[MODERATE− | B2C small business and nonprofits | 2015; click-based] Constant Contact**: analysis of 2.1M+ customer emails, counting only campaigns sent to more than 100 subscribers.
  - *Overall:* the **highest click-through came at ~20 lines of text and ≤3 images**.
  - *By sector:* for-profits peaked at 20 lines, nonprofits at 25. Retail had the narrowest window: CTR rose 50%+ between 17 and 19 lines, then fell 50% between 19 and 21. Business and service companies peaked at 13–16 images, restaurants and salons/spas at 15.

  The original press release returned HTTP 403, so these figures come from the release's indexed text and secondary coverage. — [Constant Contact press release](https://www.constantcontact.com/news/2015-03-03-constant-contact-data-reveals-direct-correlation-between-email-campaign-effectiveness-and-number-of-images-and-text-lines-featured); [Civil Society](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/charity-email-campaigns-most-successful-if-they-have-exactly-25-lines-of-text--research-finds.html)
- **[STRONG (meta-analysis) but heterogeneous | SURVEY] Cochrane (Edwards et al. 2023)**, electronic questionnaires: 32 studies, 27 strategies.
  - *About the email itself:* **picture included in the email, OR 3.05 (1.84–5.06; I²=19%; few trials, wide CI)**; white background, OR 1.31 (1.10–1.56); simple header, OR 1.23 (1.03–1.48); **male signature, OR 0.55 (0.38–0.80)**.
  - *About the ask:* **shorter questionnaire, OR 1.73 (1.40–2.13)**; **deadline, OR 1.18 (1.03–1.34)**; **statement that others had responded, OR 1.52 (1.36–1.70)**; more interesting topic, OR 1.85 (1.52–2.26); offer of the results, OR 1.36 (1.15–1.61); non-monetary incentive, OR 1.72 (1.09–2.72; I²=95%).
  - The review flags substantial heterogeneity for half the electronic strategies.

  — [Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/pt/MR000008/METHOD_metodos-para-aumentar-resposta-questionarios-postais-e-eletronicos)
- **[MODERATE− | link-building outreach] Backlinko (2019):** social-profile links in the signature go with **+9.8% response** overall. The per-network figures in the summary (Twitter +8.2%, LinkedIn +11.5%, Instagram +23.4%) don't reconcile with the body text, which says Facebook links had no effect. — [Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study)
- **[MODERATE] Link and content configuration:** Ansari & Mela (2003), the model-based +62% click-throughs from content targeting; see section 1. — [Duke PDF](https://faculty.fuqua.duke.edu/~mela/bio/papers/Ansari_Mela_2003.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Stated preference ≠ behaviour.** In HubSpot's data, two-thirds said they preferred designed emails, yet plainer emails won every behavioural test. This directly supports the MarketingOS stance that form decisions belong in code and gates rather than to a judge's taste. A model asked which email it "prefers" may behave like those survey respondents; the repo already records the judge twice preferring the wall of text.
- **Format depends on segment.** [B2B-COLD] and [B2B-NURTURE]: default to plain or lightly formatted text. [B2C] product categories: images are part of the offer (Constant Contact peaks at 13–16 images for some sectors, correlational). The Cochrane picture effect (OR 3.05) comes from survey invitations with few trials; it suggests a relevant human image *may* help a request, not that images help marketing email.
- **CTA convergence (inference).** Gong's interest CTA, Cochrane's shorter questionnaire (OR 1.73) and deadline (OR 1.18), and Boomerang's 1–3 questions all point the same way: **the cheaper and clearer the first step, the more people take it**. Gong's reply-vs-meeting gap warns that a cheap step has to lead somewhere real.
- **"One ask".** No rigorous email study of single vs multiple CTAs was found. The repo's evaluation already treats "a second ask beside the first" as a hard mutation. That framing is defensible as a principle (choice cost, as in the CTA convergence above), but it is not an empirically measured email effect. Keep it as judge and critic territory (c), or as a soft count (a), not a cited fact.
- **Applications.**
  - (a) Count distinct asks, links and image references (warn when a cold first touch has more than one distinct ask or many links); count images in `render_html` output for B2B.
  - (b) Strategist picks the format by segment. Writer uses an interest-based question for first cold touches, and states the one next step and its cost (time, effort) for B2C.
  - (c) Critic: "What exactly am I asked to do, and how much does it cost me?"
  - (d) Every CTA-variant comparison.

### Gaps
- **Single vs multiple CTAs**: the circulating claim "single CTA → +371% clicks, +1,617% sales" could not be traced to a primary source (search budget exhausted). WEAK-FOLKLORE.
- **Button vs text link**: no primary evidence retrieved; the "+28% CTR for bulletproof buttons" claim is unverified. WEAK-FOLKLORE.
- **Video thumbnails**: no primary evidence found; the "video in email lifts CTR 200–300%" claims have no traceable method. WEAK-FOLKLORE.
- **P.S.**: no email-specific evidence found. The "P.S. is read first" belief comes from direct-mail lore, not from email measurement. WEAK-FOLKLORE.
- No test of **link position or order** was retrieved (Ansari & Mela model configuration, but their exact position findings weren't re-verified).
- No B2B-nurture test since 2014 of plain text vs HTML measured on clicks, conversions or MPP-clean data.

## 5. Frequency, fatigue and unsubscribes; timing; triggered/lifecycle emails; follow-ups in sequences

### Takeaway
The best academic evidence on frequency (one firm) says the right cadence is **customer-specific**, and that **sending too few emails can cost more lifetime profit than sending too many**, although "too frequent" is consumers' top *stated* reason for unsubscribing. Send-time effects are small, inconsistent and mostly open-based, which MPP has broken. Triggered emails have randomized support (a browse-abandonment holdout) and dominate per-send revenue, but much of that comes from targeting high-intent people. In cold sequences, **follow-ups produce roughly 40–60% of all replies**, with diminishing returns beyond about 3–5 touches. A survey RCT shows each of three reminders adds response, and the gap before the first reminder (7 vs 21 days) does not matter.

### Cited Findings
- **[MODERATE | B2C retail program, one anonymous firm | structural model] Zhang, Kumar & Cosguner (2017), *JMR* 54(6):851–866.**
  - *Optimal frequency:* 5–14 emails per month depending on the customer's state, 7 per month on average. **Sending 4 (or 10) emails a month could lose 32% (or 16%) of lifetime profit**, so under-sending is worse than over-sending.
  - *Short term:* more emails raise purchases up to a threshold, then lower them.
  - *Long term:* emails move low-engagement customers to medium and keep medium ones there, but high-engagement customers may drift down.
  - *Metrics:* email-active customers are not necessarily active buyers, and judging by open rates is "short-sighted".
  - *Data:* the AMA summary gives no customer count. One secondary blog claims "53,000 emails, 5,300 purchases, 200 customers at a home-improvement store over 3 years", while Zhang's dissertation describes a "retailer's large-scale email program", so the description of the data is unresolved.

  — [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/academics/Pages/The-Sweet-Spot-in-Email-Marketing.aspx); [ISB eprints](https://eprints.exchange.isb.edu/id/eprint/520/); [GSU dissertation](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/marketing_diss/32); [ScienceSays (secondary, conflicting)](https://app.sciencesays.com/p/the-optimal-email-marketing-frequency)
- **[MODERATE | permission marketing | copula model] Kumar, Zhang & Luo (2014), *JMR* 51(4):403–419.** Jointly models opt-in time, opt-out time and average transaction amount. Marketing intensity affects when customers opt in and opt out; customer traits shift both likelihoods; and allocating resources strategically can lengthen time to opt-out while raising spending. No effect sizes were retrieved. — [GSU dissertation record](https://scholarworks.gsu.edu/marketing_diss/32)
- **[STRONG] Name in the subject line cut unsubscribes by 17% (1.2% → 1.0%)**, Sahni et al. (2018). — [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/open-name-why-personalized-email-subject-lines-work)
- **[MODERATE | subscription service, randomized field experiment; n not retrieved] "Does Customer Email Engagement Improve Profitability? A one-month engagement of **two emails half a month apart raised the likelihood of subscriber retention by 7.4% five months later**, cut churn odds by 26.3% and raised per-period consumption by 7.0%. - **Timing**

  - **[MODERATE] Kooti et al. (2015):** see section 3. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.00704)
- **Triggered and behavioural email**
  - **[STRONG design (randomized holdout); magnitude not retrieved | B2C multichannel retailer] Goic et al. (2021), *Journal of Interactive Marketing* 55:118–145**, "The Effectiveness of Triggered Email Marketing in Addressing Browse Abandonments."   - **[MODERATE (single RCT, secondary summary) | SURVEY] Sauermann & Roach (2013), *Research Policy* 42(1):273–286.** Randomized contact-design experiment with more than 24,000 people (scientists and engineers). **Each of three successive reminders significantly increased response.** No significant difference between sending the first reminder 7 or 21 days after the invitation.   - **[MODERATE− | B2B-COLD] Gong:** "I never heard back" raises replies but lowers meetings booked by 14%. — [Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/cold-email-stats)

### Inferences
- **Frequency is a profit trade-off, not a fatigue problem.** Unsubscribes are a visible cost of sending more; purchases lost by sending less are invisible. That may be why consumers' stated reasons (67% "too frequent") point one way and the structural model (under-sending costs 32% vs over-sending 16%, one firm) points the other. MarketingOS cannot optimize cadence (d), but the strategist can plan a sequence's density and justify each email's reason to exist (b).
- **Timing is (d) and low-value.** Effects are small and contradictory (Belkins finds a time-of-day effect, Hunter finds no day effect), and send-time optimization based on opens is broken by MPP. MarketingOS does not send, so at most it can suggest "weekday business hours" for B2B in the plan.
- **Triggered emails.** The causal case rests on Goic's randomized holdout. The 2%-of-sends → 30%-of-revenue and 2.11%-vs-0.16% placed-order comparisons mostly reflect *who* receives a triggered email (high-intent people), not copy. For MarketingOS this argues for writing lifecycle and trigger templates (welcome, browse and cart abandonment, back-in-stock) with content tied to the trigger. Goic's results suggest using the browsing history and recommending a wider assortment (b).
- **Follow-ups are where much of the reply volume lives:** 58.6% of replies after step 1 (Belkins); 3.3% → 6.8% going from one to three messages (Hunter); +65.8% for one follow-up (Backlinko). The datasets agree on direction, roughly agree on 3–5 total touches, and offer no evidence on spacing in days; the only randomized evidence finds no difference between 7 and 21 days. Gong's "I never heard back" result argues that each follow-up should bring a **new reason** (new proof, angle or resource) rather than a nudge.
- **Applications.**
  - (b) Strategist: cold sequences of 3–5 touches, each brief owning a distinct angle or proof.
  - (a) Deterministic near-duplicate check between the bodies of follow-ups; count sequence steps and warn beyond ~5–6 for cold.
  - (c) The existing whole-sequence read can ask: "Does each email give a new reason to act?"
  - (d) Unsubscribe and complaint cost per extra step.

### Gaps
- No randomized evidence was retrieved on **welcome/onboarding or win-back** sequence design, only Omnisend's share figures for welcome emails.
- No data on **unsubscribe or spam-complaint rates by follow-up count** (Belkins' page lacks them).
- No evidence on spacing between cold follow-ups, beyond the survey RCT's 7 vs 21 days.
- No B2B newsletter or nurture frequency study.
- Effect sizes and sample size for Goic et al. (2021), and the sample size for the car-wash MSOM experiment.
- Klaviyo flow-specific benchmarks (e.g. abandoned-cart flows reportedly at 50.5% open and 6.25% click across 143K flows) were seen only on a secondary site (Prospeo); unverified.

## 6. Current benchmark ranges (open, click, CTOR, reply, unsubscribe, spam complaint) — B2B vs B2C, by industry, with years and the open-rate inflation caveat

### Takeaway
Post-MPP open rates (≈30–45%) are not comparable with pre-MPP ones (≈17–22%), and the jump is mostly measurement. The comparable signals are **clicks** (B2C campaigns ≈1.7–2.6%, automated flows ≈5.6%), **orders** (campaigns 0.16% vs flows 2.11% placed-order rate in Klaviyo's 2026 data) and **unsubscribes** (≈0.15–0.22% per campaign in newsletter and promotional programs; 1.6% for cold sequences). Cold-email reply "benchmarks" run from 0.45% to 8.5% purely because of definitions. **No sourced spam-complaint benchmark was retrieved.**

### Inferences
- **B2B-ish vs B2C.** In Mailchimp's table, Business & Finance clicks more than E-commerce (2.78% vs 1.74%) and unsubscribes less (0.15% vs 0.19%). B2C e-commerce campaigns run around 1.7% click and 0.16% orders per recipient; automated flows run 3–13× higher (Klaviyo, Omnisend).
- **CTOR is now biased downward** because MPP inflates its denominator (opens). MailerLite's 6.81% (2025) should not be compared with pre-2021 CTORs.
- **Never quote an "average open rate" without its year and MPP status.** Before 2021, ~17–22% was typical (Constant Contact, 17.6% in 2021). From 2023 to 2025, 30–45% is typical.
- **For MarketingOS** these are (d), context only. They could anchor expectations in the UI or report ("a promotional campaign clicking at 2% is ordinary"), but they cannot evaluate a draft.

- No B2B newsletter or nurture-specific benchmark source was found beyond Mailchimp's industry rows.

## 7. How people actually read emails — mobile share, scanning, time spent, dark mode, what readers look at first — and the resulting evidence-based anatomy

### Takeaway
Readers **triage in seconds**: about 9–13 seconds of average attention per opened email in Litmus' pixel data (9 s in 2022), only ~19% of newsletters read fully (NN/g eyetracking), introductions largely skipped, attention concentrated on the first words of headlines. Surveys put the **sender name first, then subject, then preview text**, but no experiment isolating sender or preheader was found. Mobile is the main reading environment (42% of opens in 2018, "majority" per Litmus in 2026), and more than 25% of Litmus' user base uses dark mode. Under load, people reply to fewer emails and write shorter replies.

### Inferences
- **Reading model for the copy (inference from the above).** People triage on the inbox row (sender → subject → preview). Most opened emails then get seconds, not minutes. Body copy is scanned from the top: openings are often skipped and headline starts get fixated. So the point, and why it matters to this reader, have to survive a few seconds of scanning on a phone. That is consistent with the existing `clarity_gate` (the offering must be named where the reader looks) and with a comprehension-based veto.
- **Post-MPP read-time data is likely biased** toward non-Apple clients. MPP-cached opens can't be timed, so they presumably drop out of "read time" measurement. This is an inference; Litmus doesn't document it.
- **Dark mode and mobile** are rendering concerns for `render_html` (contrast, image backgrounds) rather than copy concerns. Mobile mainly argues for short paragraphs and an early point.

#### What MarketingOS can use now vs. what needs send data
- **Deterministic code, (a), defensible as gates:** an empty subject; merge-field syntax and fallbacks. These are supported by mechanism or the evidence above.
- **Deterministic code, (a), as soft signals rather than blocks:** word-count bands per type, question count, number of asks and links, readability in the right language, near-duplicate follow-ups, step count. The evidence for these is correlational and modest, so blocking on them would contradict "only code stops a draft, and only for what can be checked".
- **Prompt instructions, (b):** relevance with a reason-why; plain format for B2B; an interest-based first ask for cold email; one proof; each follow-up with a new angle; trigger-specific content.
- **Cold-reader and critic criteria, (c):** "What is this, and why me?" from the inbox row; "Can I answer in one line, and is the next step worth it?"; "Does it reveal unexplained knowledge about me?"; "Does each email in the sequence add a reason?" Form preferences (designed vs plain, longer vs shorter) should stay out of the judge; HubSpot's stated-vs-behavioural gap and the repo's own judge-bench finding both point that way.
- **Only with real send data, (d):** the size of every effect above; subject-line wording; timing; frequency; CTA variants; anything open-based.
