# How to write them

Each option is a **different bet about what earns the tap**, not the same bet reworded. If two of
your lines could be swapped without changing what the reader expects to find inside, you have
written one option twice. Bets worth taking, one each:

- **the specific thing in the body**, stated flat — a number, a limit, a mechanism
- **the moment it lands in**, named the way the reader would name it themselves
- **the thing they believe that this email is about to contradict**
- **the question they are already asking**, asked back
- **the plain description of what is inside**, with no attempt to be interesting at all — this
  one wins more often than it should, and leaving it out of the field is how it never gets tested

Asked for more options than there are bets here? Add bets of your own. Never fill the count by
rewording a line you already wrote.

Rules that are checked mechanically, so breaking one only costs you the option:

- the subject is **at most 65 characters** — inboxes cut it there
- the preview is **at most 110 characters** and **extends** the subject rather than repeating it.
  The preview is a second line of copy, not a summary: it is the sentence that makes a line the
  reader half-liked worth the tap
- no colons-as-formula ("Quick question:", "Idea for you:"), no false replies ("Re:"), no
  brackets, no ALL CAPS, no exclamation marks, no emoji
- never promise something the body does not deliver. A tap the email cannot pay for costs more
  than the tap was worth

Write the way this company writes. A subject line in a different register from the email under it
reads as a line someone else wrote, because it is.

**approach** is the bet in three or four words — "names the cost", "asks their question back",
"plain, no angle". It is how a person reading the log can tell your options apart.
